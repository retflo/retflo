# The Articles of Confederation: The Dial, Not the Verdict

## Position

The critic's cleanest natural experiment: a federation of self-governing units, peacetime, no foreign conqueror — and within six years its own members replaced it with a sovereign state because it couldn't tax, couldn't coordinate, couldn't act. "Nobody crushed it. It centralized itself, by deliberation, in the calm."

It's a real data point, and the framework engages it rather than dodging it. Three counters, in ascending order of force:

### 1. The Failure Was Task-Specific

The Articles failed at enumerable functions: servicing war debt, coordinating interstate commerce, funding common obligations. The diagnosis of 1787 was not "horizontalism fails" — it was "these specific coordination functions lack capacity." The remedy matched the diagnosis: centralize the failing functions, then surround the centralization with checks, divisions, enumerated powers, and multiple veto points. "Centralize only enough to solve specific coordination failures, under maximum feasible constraint" is the federation design principle, demonstrated — not refuted — by the episode. → `auth/federation-vs-state.md`

### 2. The Replacement Proves the Dial Exists

What replaced the Articles was not a unitary sovereign. It was a federal compromise — radically decentralized by the standards of every contemporary European state, with competing jurisdictions, divided authority, and reserved powers. 1787 chose a *point on the centralization dial*. The existence of the dial — that coordination capacity is a continuous design variable, settable per function, adjustable under constraint — is the framework's entire claim about authority. The critic needs 1787 to prove the dial has only one usable setting. It proved people argue about the setting, which they have done continuously since.

### 3. The Test Case Was Never Bottom-Up Federation

The Articles federated thirteen elite-governed states — propertied legislatures, restricted franchises, slaveholding ruling classes — not communities practicing distributed governance internally. And ratification of the replacement was a contested elite project: creditors holding depreciated war debt, panic over Shays' Rebellion, ratifying conventions chosen on restricted franchises, anti-federalist defeats by narrow margins in key states. A society did not serenely deliberate its way to the discovery that horizontalism fails; a specific coalition with specific material interests built a stronger center partly to enforce debt collection and suppress agrarian revolt. That history is evidence about how centralization actually happens — who drives it and what it protects — and it runs the framework's direction. → `hist/capitalism-origins.md`

### Deploy In Combination

Any counter alone can be picked at; together they reframe the episode. The Articles show that federated designs must include adequate capacity for genuinely shared functions — a design lesson the framework's federation model already incorporates (delegated, mandated, recallable capacity is capacity). What the Articles cannot show is that the capacity must take the form of a permanent sovereign monopoly, because what was actually built in 1787 was itself an argument about scoping, dividing, and leashing the center — conducted by people who feared concentration enough to engineer against it even while creating it.

## Objection Handling

| Move | Response | Concession |
|---|---|---|
| "It failed in peacetime with no external enemy — your best excuse is gone" | Conceded: no conqueror. What failed was task-specific capacity — debt, trade, common funding. The remedy was scoped centralization under heavy constraint, which is the federation principle in action. A design that under-provisions shared functions and then gets re-provisioned is evidence about provisioning, not about the impossibility of federation. | **Fact** — concedes the failure was functional, not total — the dispute becomes which functions need what capacity, a design question |
| "Its own members chose centralization — revealed preference" | A restricted-franchise elite coalition, motivated by debt enforcement and Shays' panic, chose it through contested conventions and near-miss ratifications. That's a finding about who gets to do the choosing when centers get built. Revealed preference arguments need the choosers to be the governed. | **Fact** — concedes the centralization was an interested political project — which undermines its use as a neutral verdict on governance forms |
| "1787 proves you need a sovereign center" | 1787 produced a divided, enumerated, checked, federal center that its own architects described as an experiment in preventing concentration. If it proves anything, it proves the builders agreed the center is dangerous and the leash is the work. The dial exists; everything since is a fight over the setting. | **Fact** — concedes the constitutional project itself treats concentration as the hazard — the framework's premise, stated by the critic's own example |
| "You're explaining away the cleanest counterexample" | It's engaged at full strength: real failure, real peacetime, real voluntary re-centralization — all conceded as facts. What's refused is the inference from "this federation under-provisioned shared capacity" to "all distributed governance is impossible." The gap between those two claims is where the entire argument lives, and the episode doesn't close it. | **Frame** — concedes the dispute is over the inference, not the history — facts granted, conclusion still unowned |
